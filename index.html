Who Gets the Wall?

Flood levees, noise barriers, reservations, national parks. Each one is a story about who gets protection and who gets property rights. Play with the numbers below and see where the benefits actually land.

By Tom Hamilton. Built from my written answers and audio discussion for Week Nine.

Indianapolis · August 2026

The levee test

In August 2026, a record White River flood hit Indianapolis. The North levee kept the river out of Broad Ripple. A mile away, the town of Rocky Ripple sat outside the wall and sandbagged its own 1937 levee. The Army Corps funds a levee only if its benefit-cost ratio is above 1, and it counts benefits mostly as property damage avoided. Try it yourself.

Simplified model: benefit = buildings × home value × a damage factor calibrated so Rocky Ripple's real numbers (about 315 buildings, $33.5M extra cost) give the 0.3 ratio in the city's 2017 study. Illustrative, not the Corps' actual model.

The catch: drag the home value up and Rocky Ripple passes, with nothing else changed. The rule measures protection in dollars of property, so it flows to where wealth already is. Rocky Ripple is mostly white with income near the state median, so a demographic EJ law like Massachusetts' would never flag it.

Who keeps the quiet?

When a highway noise barrier goes up, nearby home prices jump about 6.8%. Traffic noise costs the U.S. an estimated $110 billion, borne most by lower-income households. So a barrier is environmental justice, right? Pick who you are.

The proposal

Give the national parks back?

Yellowstone took Crow land. Glacier erased Blackfeet hunting rights. Yosemite pushed out the Ahwahneechee. Meanwhile, parks are crowded, underpriced, and carry a maintenance backlog of over $20 billion. One proposal: hand them to the tribes that were removed, with a covenant like "no Costco at Old Faithful."

The case for

  • Parks are already federal land, so no homeowner is displaced.
  • One owner of a whole park has every reason to protect the view visitors pay for.
  • Tribal parks already price for crowding: Ute Mountain charges $50 a person a day, Yellowstone $35 a car.
  • Cheap access mostly subsidizes visitors who can afford a car, time off, and travel.

The case against

  • Under the trust system, tribes may not get clear title, so they'd face the same federal approvals that stall reservations.
  • Several tribes claim the same parks. Who decides?
  • Higher prices raise their own access concern for low-income visitors.
  • Owners could close access entirely, as Uluru's owners banned climbing.

05

The common thread

Rocky Ripple, noise barriers, reservations, and parks share one lesson: environmental justice runs through property rights. Casinos boomed and the Squamish Nation is building thousands of homes in Vancouver because tribes got a clear right to valuable land. A land acknowledgment gives a tribe its name back. A real property right gives it power.
