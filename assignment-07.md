The Arctic Grid Fund: Drill ANWR, Endow America's Clean Energy Grid

Should all federal oil and gas revenue from the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge's Coastal Plain go into a permanent, diversified Arctic Grid Fund? Withdraw no more than 4% of its value each year, in this order of priority: at least 50% for electric transmission and grid upgrades, at least 30% for energy storage and firm clean power, and the remainder for offshore wind, with annual public audits.


What's the deal?
We lease the ANWR's 1.56-million-acre Coastal Plain to drilling and bank the proceeds forever. At 16 billion barrels and today's ~$89, the oil is worth $1.4 trillion, but Washington's royalty share is about $71 billion, collected over decades. Invested like a university endowment and spending 4% a year, it yields about $2.8 billion annually. As a result of this plan, we can develop a grid that carries clean power. Each year, half the money upgrades and builds transmission for clean energy, 30% will fund batteries and firm clean power, and the rest will go to offshore wind developments. In exchange, we give up the untouched land of the ANWR. This is the Porcupine caribou herd's calving grounds, America's most important polar bear denning land, and home to several endangered species. Also, 6.9 billion tons of CO₂ will be created when the oil burns.
Who Wins: electricity customers, clean-energy developers, and the climate. 
Who Loses: Arctic wildlife, certain Indigenous Nations, and wilderness lovers. 

The best argument for voting yes:
Drilling in ANWR is already happening: federal law now requires four lease sales by 2035, and the first was held in June 2026. The real question now isn't whether to drill, but what the money will go to. This proposal seeks to put these funds towards something environmentally conscious. It also targets the bottleneck currently present with green energy. Only 13% of capacity requesting interconnection between 2000 and 2020 has come online. This Grid money can unlock private projects that are ready and waiting to go, multiplying every public dollar. ANWR's oil will mostly replace barrels produced elsewhere, often under weaker environmental rules, so the net climate cost is far smaller than the gross. Meanwhile, $2.8 billion a year in grid and storage investment keeps paying for decades after the last well is capped. If carefully managed, this deal could buy decades of green energy development in exchange for one piece of land.

The best argument for voting no:
Burning 16 billion barrels releases roughly 6.9 billion tons of CO₂, which is more than a full year of total U.S. emissions. It also locks in Arctic oil infrastructure for 40-plus years, exactly when the world needs to wind it down. SO while this deal is intended to make green energy more viable, it could directly fund the fossil fuels industry for decades. What we lose is hard to put a price on. The coastal plain is the Porcupine caribou herd's calving ground and the most important land denning habitat for polar bears. It is also considered sacred by certain indigenous nations. And money isn't what's holding back the energy grid. The real issues are transmission stalls on permitting and siting. As a result, a giant fund could sit idle or flow to politically favored projects, as future Congresses can raid any trust fund they wish. We'd be making a certain, permanent sacrifice for an uncertain, revocable benefit.

Where is the body buried? (weakest part of the argument)
The weakest number is the total quantity of barrels. Sixteen billion is an optimistic estimate; BLM's range is 4.25 to 11.8 billion, and at the midpoint the fund halves to about $1.4 billion a year. Also, Industry interest is thin. June 2026's sale of drilling rights drew only two bidders. Also, the payoff is not immediate. Drilling will only commence roughly eight years after a sale, so the fund won't fill until the mid-2030s. And in the meantime, oil prices could fluctuate. Finally, Alaska keeps 70% of royalties, which is more than double Washington's share. Therefore, the fund is capped by weak royalties.

Why isn't this already happening?
Alaska's delegation fought for decades to open ANWR and won a 50–70% revenue share, and Alaska has every reason to block the redirection of funds. Also, Oil companies care about lease terms, and the current incentives may not be strong enough to start drilling. On top of that, many environmental groups treat ANWR as a line they won't trade. Endorsing drilling, even for green money, would split their donors. Drilling supporters don't want oil revenue earmarked for wind and transmission.  Budget rules also work against it. Congress has used ANWR revenue to offset tax bills; a locked endowment removes money politicians can spend now. Dedicated trust funds also invite future raids, so promises lack credibility. Finally, government policy has frequently flipped. ANWR leasing was authorized in 2017, paused in 2021, canceled in 2023, and mandated again in 2025, so it has a very uncertain future.
